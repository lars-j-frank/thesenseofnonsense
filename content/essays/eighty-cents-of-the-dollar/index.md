*An essay from the ledger. Sources at the bottom.*

---

If you read the name alone, Natural Resources Conservation Board, you picture a tribunal that weighs forests, reservoirs, and ski hills against the public interest. That work is real. The Board still does it. Four active natural-resource files were on its project list when I pulled the site, and its Springbank Off-Stream Reservoir decision from 2021 is a thick public-interest report of the old school.

I opened the audited financial statements anyway, because that is the hobby.

The Statement of Operations for the year ended March 31, 2025, splits the agency's directly incurred expenses into two lines. Board reviews and hearings: $1,084,168. Regulating confined feeding operations: $4,824,262. Total: $5,908,430.

That is 18 per cent on the Board line and 82 per cent on confined feeding. Look back a few years and the ratio barely moves. In 2023-24 it was 18 and 82. In 2022-23, 19 and 81. In 2019-20, 21 and 79. For the stretch of statements I could line up cleanly, about four of every five operating dollars go to the Agricultural Operation Practices Act side of the house: permits, inspections, science support, compliance.

The Board line, for what it is worth, covers both AOPA appeals and the Natural Resources Conservation Board Act project reviews. The footnotes say so. Science and technical work that supports both mandates mostly sits under the confined-feeding line. So the public-interest tribunal is even thinner on the page than the 18 per cent suggests, once you notice where SciTech parks.

---

Revenue is simpler than the name.

In 2024-25 the NRCB recognized $6,076,000 as a transfer from the Department of Environment and Protected Areas, plus $104,608 of investment income and $3,412 of other revenue. On the face of the statements, that transfer is the business. The NRCB collects it quarterly. Put beside sister agencies on the same policy shelf, the design difference is the source line: AER draws an industry levy; ERA draws TIER Fund architecture; the NRCB draws Environment and Protected Areas general revenue.

Accommodation is the one large cost that does not appear in the Statement of Operations total. Schedule 4 allocates $434,490 of space costs by square footage for 2024-25, bringing the all-in figure to about $6.34 million. Same pattern as other Alberta agencies that sit in government buildings and get the rent billed elsewhere.

Salaries, wages, and benefits were $5,038,005 of the $5.9 million. This is a people agency.

---

Then there is the surplus, which is the closest thing here to a float, and it behaves like appropriated money that can be recalled.

Accumulated surplus sat at $3,998,522 on March 31, 2020. A year later it was $1,587,245. The notes explain the drop: the budgeted transfer from Environment and Parks was $5,397,000; an adjustment of $2,697,000 cut the actual transfer to $2,700,000. The agency posted a $2.41 million deficit for the year because the ministry took the cushion back.

Smaller call-backs show up too. The 2018-19 transfer originally booked at $6,433,000 had $1,300,000 returned. In 2021-22 the Board returned $250,000 after an underspend, which the statements describe as fiscal prudence. By March 31, 2025, accumulated surplus had climbed again to $2,516,559, with $2,657,025 of cash on the statement of financial position.

That is the surplus dynamic: general-revenue money that can pool and can be pulled. Readers of this site who have followed the TIER Fund know a float measured in hundreds of millions and tied to industrial carbon payments. The NRCB's cushion lives in the low millions and answers to the ministry that writes the transfer.

---

Compensation disclosure for 2024-25 puts the Chair at $259,277 total and the chief executive officer at $252,547. Part-time Board members land around $123,000 to $127,000. The Chair position is disclosed as 80 per cent permanent part-time; the other Board seats at 60 per cent. Sandi Roberts became Chair in December 2024 after years as a Board member and, earlier, as an NRCB approval officer. Sean Royer is CEO. Peter Woloshyn, who had been CEO and then Chair across roughly eighteen years, stepped down the same month.

None of that tells you whether a particular feedlot decision was right. It tells you what kind of organization this is on the money side: a small regulator whose payroll and transfer dominate the page, whose conservation-board brand shares a budget line with AOPA appeals, and whose surplus lives at the pleasure of the department that funds it.

---

I will stop before the requests-for-review statistics, the Springbank intervener cost awards, and the Policy Advisory Group seating chart. Those are the next ledgers. For a first pass, the operating statements are enough.

Eighty cents of the dollar, give or take a couple of points depending on the year, buy confined feeding regulation paid from Environment and Protected Areas. The rest buys the Board. The name on the letterhead is longer than that. The statement of operations is not.
